# §1 · Audience Psychology Diagnosis

## Why partially-paid customers have not completed

The part payment is the most informative thing about this audience. It is not a
half-hearted yes. It is a **complete emotional yes followed by an incomplete
rational one** — and the gap between those two is where every one of these
customers is currently sitting.

Somebody said yes in a room (physical or Zoom) that was warm, social, and had
an inviter they trusted standing next to the decision. Then the room closed.
Nothing in their normal week reproduces that room. The conviction was never
transferred out of that context and into their own reasoning, so it decayed on
schedule.

### The eight real causes, ranked by how many people they explain

**1 · The open tab (largest single cause).**
A part payment is psychologically comfortable in a way that neither zero nor
full payment is. They have *done something*. The item is off the guilt list
without being on the done list. Full payment closes a loop; zero payment closes
a loop; a part payment leaves an open tab, and open tabs are restful. There is
nothing in the current process that makes the open tab uncomfortable, so it
stays open indefinitely. **This is a process defect, not a customer defect.**

**2 · Inviter dependency.**
The relationship is with the person who invited them, not with Welocity. If
that person went quiet — got busy, changed focus, left the network — the
customer's entire connection to the programme went quiet with them. A large
share of this list is almost certainly not "objecting" at all. They are
*orphaned*. Test this before assuming anything else: cross-tab the pending list
by inviter and look for clusters.

**3 · They cannot picture what arrives.**
"A 150-page report" is not something a human being can want. Nobody has ever
lain awake wanting a PDF. They bought a *feeling* (finally understanding my own
body) and the deliverable they can picture is *paperwork*. The imagination gap
between those two is where urgency died.

**4 · Post-purchase dissonance, sharpened by the emotional yes.**
The more emotionally a decision is made, the sharper the next-morning "what did
I just do?" Some of this list went home, told a spouse, heard "you paid how
much for a spit test?", and has been quietly embarrassed ever since. They are
not avoiding the balance. They are avoiding the conversation about the balance.

**5 · Price re-anchoring after the fact.**
They searched online. They found consumer DNA kits at a fraction of the price.
They now suspect they overpaid, and nobody has explained the difference between
a raw-data kit and an interpreted, counselled programme. **The brief does not
list this objection. It is one of the top three.** Left unanswered it does not
present as a price objection — it presents as silence, because people do not
like saying "I think I was overcharged" to the person who sold them something.

**6 · Information avoidance (the ostrich effect).**
Real, well-documented, and consistently under-diagnosed by sales teams because
nobody volunteers it. It is strongest in exactly the people with the most family
history — that is, the people the programme is most relevant to. They will
present as "busy". They are not busy.

**7 · Trust decay across a long, invisible process.**
Saliva goes into an envelope, travels to Mumbai, and nothing happens for three
weeks. There is no tracking, no acknowledgement in the mental model. For a
customer who has already paid part of a five-figure sum to a company they met
once on Zoom, three weeks of silence is not a wait — it is a risk they have not
yet decided to take.

**8 · Genuine affordability.**
Smaller than the team assumes, because it is the most socially acceptable reason
to give and therefore the most over-reported. Some of it is real and must be
respected without a flinch. Most of it is one of the seven above, wearing the
one costume that ends a conversation politely.

### The diagnostic the team should run before the session

Two data cuts change how the session is delivered:

- **Time since part payment.** Under 60 days is a stalled sale. Over 12 months
  is a dormant relationship, and a session is the wrong instrument — that
  person needs a one-to-one and possibly an exit offer.
- **Contact since part payment: any / none.** The "none" group is the orphaned
  cohort. Their objection is almost never the product.

Bring these two numbers into the room. They change which objection branches in
§20 you rehearse.

### What must be true at minute 60

Not "they were convinced." They were already convinced once. What must be true
is that **the original conviction has been rebuilt on rational ground**, so it
survives the drive home, the spouse, and Tuesday morning.

# §2 · Session Strategy

## §2.1 How this differs from *Know Your DNA*

| | *Know Your DNA* (cold) | **Merit Session** (part-paid) |
|---|---|---|
| Job to be done | Awareness → first yes | Reconnection → value clarity → objection resolution → completion |
| Audience state | Never considered it | Considered it, decided yes, stalled |
| DNA basics | ~30 min, foundational | ~7 min, refresher only |
| Emotional engine | Curiosity + concern | Self-recognition + resolution of an open loop |
| Proof needed | Science is real | **Process is real** and my sample is safe |
| Price | Revealed with a value anchor | Anchor removed; balance handled one-to-one |
| Celebrity content | Central | Removed entirely |
| Fear content | Present | Removed entirely |
| Central question | "Should I know this?" | "Why haven't I finished something I already chose?" |
| Success | Orders placed | Dated commitments — including honest exits |

## §2.2 The strategic inversion

*Know Your DNA* persuades. The Merit Session **hands the persuasion back**.

These people have already been persuaded once. Persuading them again implies
their first decision was wrong, which is quietly insulting and puts them on the
defensive about a payment they already made. The correct posture is that of
someone helping them **audit their own earlier decision** — a decision you treat
as intelligent, because they made it.

The presenter's job is not to be right. It is to be **the person who was
relaxed about them saying no.** That posture is what makes the yes stick.

## §2.3 Three governing rules

**Rule 1 — Never mention the money they already paid.**
Not once. Not as "you've already invested". Not as "you've come this far". The
brief bans sunk-cost manipulation, and the ban only means anything if the prior
payment is *invisible* on stage. It is their money and their business. The
moment it is named, every sentence after it reads as pressure.

**Rule 2 — Name the delay before they defend it.**
People defend positions they have been attacked on. If the presenter says
"most people here haven't finished, and there are seven normal reasons why,
and I've delayed things myself" *before* anyone feels accused, the defensive
posture never forms and the reflection in Act 11 is real instead of performed.

**Rule 3 — Sell the interpretation, never the report.**
The report is the artefact. The counselling session is the product. Every
value claim routes through *"what will you understand that you don't understand
now?"* This also happens to be the honest answer to the cheap-kit objection
(§20), which makes it strategically load-bearing rather than just good taste.

## §2.4 The one number that matters on the night

Not orders. **Dated commitments** — how many people leave with a specific date
and a named next action, including the ones whose action is "send me the refund
form."

Chasing orders on the night pushes the presenter into pressure at minute 57,
which destroys the trust the previous 56 minutes bought. Dated commitments
convert better over the following ten days *and* leave the brand intact.

## §2.5 Recommended: do not do the balance ask from the stage

The brief's Act 15 reveals price and individual balances on stage. Recommend
against, for three concrete reasons:

1. **Different people owe different amounts.** Displaying that in a group tells
   everyone that others got different terms. That conversation cannot be
   controlled once it starts.
2. **It is personal financial information** shown in a room that may include a
   customer's neighbour, colleague or relative.
3. **The stage cannot handle affordability with dignity.** The single most
   respectful response to "I genuinely can't right now" is a quiet private
   conversation. A stage cannot offer one.

**Route A (recommended).** On stage: what completing unlocks, the process
end-to-end, and the total programme price so nobody feels a number is being
hidden. Individual balances, payment options and dates go to a scheduled
one-to-one — booked in the room, on the night.

**Route B (only if every attendee's balance is identical and that is verified).**
Full stage reveal per the brief's Act 15. The slide is written for both routes
in §26 — Slide 17 carries an A and a B variant.

## §2.6 The dual-audience problem — and the fix

The brief asks this session to also serve "complete new potential customers."
It cannot, as written, and pretending otherwise is the single biggest risk in
the design.

**Why.** Act 1 ("remember why you said yes"), Act 11 ("why haven't I
completed?"), Act 12 (delay branches) and Act 15 (balance) all address someone
who has already paid. A cold prospect sitting through those four blocks spends
roughly eighteen minutes being addressed as someone they are not. Worse, they
learn something you would not choose to teach them on first contact: **that a
lot of people who bought this never finished it.** That is a genuinely damaging
first impression, and no amount of good storytelling repairs it.

**The fix — one session, six adaptation touches.** These are written into the
scripts in `02` and marked `[COLD]`:

| # | Where | The touch |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Slide 2, opening | "Some of you started this journey a while ago. A few of you are hearing it properly for the first time. Both of you are in the right room — the question I want to leave you with is the same one." |
| 2 | Act 1 | Frame as *"the moment you first thought this might be for you"* — which every attendee has had, paid or not |
| 3 | Act 11 | Ask "what is stopping you *today*" — works identically for a first decision and a stalled one |
| 4 | Act 12 | Delay branches phrased as reasons *people* have, never "reasons you have" |
| 5 | Slide 17 | Programme total, not balances (Route A). A cold attendee sees a clean price, not someone else's arrears |
| 6 | Slide 19 CTA | **Two lanes on the card**: "Complete my journey" and "Start from the beginning" |

**Hard limit.** If cold attendees will be more than roughly a third of the room,
run two sessions. This design degrades gracefully, not infinitely.

### Two rows that are not optional

**Row 12 (data governance)** and **Row 25 (refunds)**. Neither appears anywhere

  one person who wants their part payment back. If the presenter's honest answer

  session.** A clean refund answer is one of the most persuasive things that can
  be said in the room — and if the answer is "non-refundable," say that clearly

## Recommendation — **"The Unfinished Decision"**

Three reasons it wins.

**It is about them, not the product.** Nothing in it mentions DNA, testing,
health or payment. That is the point. A title that names the product invites
sales resistance before the first slide.

**"Unfinished" is factually accurate and carries no blame.** It describes a
state, not a failure. Nobody reads it as an accusation; everybody recognises
themselves in it.

**It generates the exact internal question the brief is aiming for.** The brief
says the audience must arrive at *"why am I still delaying something I already
decided was important?"* This title plants that question at minute zero and
lets sixty minutes answer it.

It also works, unmodified, for a cold attendee — an unfinished decision is a
decision you have not made yet.

**Subtitle for the deck:** *Sixty minutes about a choice you already made.*

# §6 · Complete Story Arc

Ten movements. One story. The audience should never feel a topic change — only
the story turning.

```
ACT 1 · THE THING WE HAVE IN COMMON              0–5
   You started something. It stopped.
   I'm not here to restart it. I'm here to help you look at it.
        ↓  lowers resistance
ACT 2 · THE THINGS WE STILL GUESS                5–12
   Coffee. Dal. Sleep. Same input, different bodies.
        ↓  from "the product" to "my body"
ACT 3 · THE MANUAL THAT NEVER CAME               12–19
   You get a manual with a car. Not with a body.
   DNA is not the whole manual — it is one important chapter.
        ↓  installs the honest scope
ACT 4 · HOW THE CHAPTER IS WRITTEN               19–26
   Cells → DNA → variants → tendencies.
   DNA is information. Not destiny.
        ↓  the trust slide
ACT 5 · FROM GENERAL ADVICE TO YOUR QUESTIONS    26–33
   "What's healthy for most people?" → "What's relevant for me?"
        ↓  relevance becomes personal
ACT 6 · WHAT IF YOU KNEW?                        33–40
   Six moments. Information → better question →
   better conversation → better-informed action.
        ↓  the emotional peak
ACT 7 · WHAT IT CANNOT DO                        40–45
   It will not fix your weight. It will not name your disease.
   Predisposition ≠ diagnosis ≠ outcome.
        ↓  trust peaks here, not earlier
ACT 8 · WHAT YOU ACTUALLY CHOSE                  45–50
   Not a report. An interpreted conversation about you.
        ↓  value clarity
ACT 9 · THE UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION               50–55
   Not "should I test?" — you answered that.
   "Why haven't I finished?" Seven honest reasons. Pick yours.
        ↓  self-recognition, privately
ACT 10 · GIVE THE DECISION A DATE                55–60
   Not "pay now". A date, a next step, or a clean exit.
```

**The spine, in one line:** *You already knew this mattered. Let's find out
whether you were right — and then either finish it or close it properly.*

**The callback:** Act 1 says "one thing you all have in common." Act 10 returns:
*"I said you had one thing in common. I was wrong. You have two. You all
started — and every one of you can still decide how this ends."*

### Deviations from the brief's timing, and why

- **Act 2 gets 7 min, not 7 min at 5–12 with basics.** Held as briefed.
- **"What if you knew?" expanded to 7 minutes.** It is the emotional peak and
  the brief calls for six full stories; five minutes forces them into a list.
- **"What it cannot do" gets a dedicated slide at minute 45.** The brief folds
  this into Act 7. Splitting it out puts the single most trust-building slide at
  the exact point where a warm audience is deciding whether the presenter is
  honest. Trust must peak *before* the value section, not after.
- **The delay section runs 4 minutes, not 7.** The brief allocates 47–54.
  Four minutes of genuine silence and self-recognition beats seven minutes of a
  presenter talking about objections. Cut the presenter's airtime, not the
  reflection.

### Two buffers to build in

- **Minute 33 is the drift point.** If running long, the six scenarios compress
  to four (drop scenarios 4 and 6 — marked in §13).
- **Never borrow time from minutes 43–47.** The limits section is where this
  session earns its credibility. Overrun into the close instead.
